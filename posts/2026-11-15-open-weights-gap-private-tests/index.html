When the Test Went Private, the Open-Weights Gap Came Back

By ylnhari · November 15, 2026 · LLMs, open weights, benchmarks, pricing

Three months ago it looked as if the open models had almost caught up. On the most widely quoted scoreboard for AI models, the best model you could download and run yourself sat just three points below the best model you could only rent. The question people were asking had moved on from "are open models good enough?" to "why pay for the expensive ones at all?"

Then the test changed. On 27 October, Artificial Analysis rebuilt its Intelligence Index and moved most of it onto questions that have never been published. The open models fell. The best of them now sits 19 points below the leader.

This post covers:

  • what changed in the test, and why that matters more than any single model launch
  • what the leading models cost to run on that test, which turns out to be a different ranking from list price
  • what "open" actually means for each model, because the licences now differ a lot
  • where businesses are really spending their money
  • which model to pick, depending on what you are building
Benchmarks that anyone can read are benchmarks that anyone can train on. Take the answers away and the leaderboard looks different.

1. The test moved to questions nobody has seen

The Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index is a single score built from a set of harder tests, covering coding, science, knowledge work and general reasoning. Version 5 is its biggest change since January. For the first time, the majority of the index is built on private datasets, and Artificial Analysis says 60% of it now rests on tests with private questions or held-out answers.

The tasks also got longer. Version 5 leans on agentic work, where a model has to plan and carry out many steps in a terminal or a codebase. Artificial Analysis reports that the median output per task rose from about 35,000 tokens to 59,000.

Its own summary of the result was blunt: "Open weights models fall further behind in v5." Here is what the new index looks like, with each model's score plotted against what it actually cost to run the test:

The top of the chart belongs to models you cannot download. Claude Fable 5.2 leads at 65, followed by Gemini 4 Carbon at 60 and Claude Opus 5.5 at 59. The best open-weights model, Kimi K3.1 Pro, scores 46. Moonshot's model had ranked fourth on the old version of the index and dropped to ninth. Gizmodo's reporting put the open-versus-closed gap at 3 points under the old index and 12 under the new one; the exact number depends on which leader you compare against, and the leaderboard has moved again since.

2. Why private questions change the answer

Every public benchmark eventually leaks into training data. Questions get discussed online, answers get scraped, and models trained on the open web end up having seen some of the test. That does not have to be deliberate to inflate a score. Private test sets are the standard defence, and the result above suggests that public tests had been flattering some models more than others.

There is a second, equally reasonable reading. Version 5 rewards long, multi-step agentic work much more heavily than before. That is exactly where the largest proprietary models have had the most post-training effort poured into them. So the reopened gap might be partly contamination washing out, and partly a genuine difference in the kind of work being measured. The data cannot separate the two, and anyone who tells you which one it is with certainty is guessing.

What both readings share is the practical lesson. A score on a public benchmark is a weaker signal than it used to be, especially for close comparisons. That is also why Artificial Analysis warns that version 5 scores cannot be compared with earlier versions, and why every number in this post comes from the new index alone.

3. What the leaders actually cost to run

List price per million tokens hides a lot. Two models with the same price can differ several-fold in how many tokens they spend to solve the same task. Artificial Analysis now publishes a measured cost per task: what it actually paid to run each model through the index. On that measure, the spread at the top is enormous. Among the ten highest-scoring models, cost per task varies by more than 13 times.

The most useful single comparison comes straight from Artificial Analysis: GPT-6.1 Sol scores within 4 points of Claude Opus 5.5 at about an eighth of the cost per task, $1.27 against $10.04. If you are paying for the very top of the range by default, that is the comparison to run on your own work.

The setting you choose matters as much as the model. Most frontier models now have an "effort" dial that trades thinking time for quality. At launch, Artificial Analysis measured that Claude Opus 5.5 scores 35 at low effort and 58 at max, at 10 times the cost per task. The same model can be a budget option or a premium one depending on one parameter.

And at the cheap end, the open models no longer win by default. Claude Haiku 5.5 scores 36 at $0.41 per task, while the best MIT-licensed open model, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, scores 33 at $0.60. On this test, a small proprietary model is both better and cheaper. That has not been true for most of the past year.

The question is no longer "open or closed". It is which model, at which effort setting, for which task.

4. "Open" now means three different things

"Open-weights model" covers a wide range of licences, and the differences matter as soon as you build a product on one.

  • Genuinely permissive (MIT). DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 ships under the MIT licence, as does GLM-5.3-Flash. No revenue triggers, no user caps, no branding rules. These are the safest to build on, and on the new index they score in the low 30s.
  • Open, with conditions for big operators. The two strongest open models carry custom licences. Kimi K3.1 Pro's licence requires a separate agreement with Moonshot once a model-as-a-service business passes $20 million of revenue in 12 months, and prominent "Kimi K3.1-Pro" branding above 100 million monthly users or $20 million monthly revenue. Internal use is exempt. GLM-5.3's licence sets its trigger far higher, at $10 billion of revenue, where providers need a security review.
  • Proprietary. No weights at all. Everything above 50 points on the new index is in this group.

For most companies, the conditions on Kimi and GLM-5.3 will never apply. But if you plan to resell inference at scale, read the licence before the benchmark.

5. Where the money is actually going

Ramp publishes a monthly AI index built from the card and bill-pay spending of tens of thousands of US businesses. It is not the whole economy, and Ramp says so, but it is one of the few views of what companies actually pay for.

Two things stand out in the latest data. First, spending is concentrated, and it is concentrated in a premium vendor: in the week ending 11 October, Anthropic took 60.1% of AI token spending among businesses on Ramp. Second, growth has slowed. In October, token spend grew 2.1% month over month for OpenAI and 0.5% for Anthropic.

So businesses are not abandoning the top of the range. They are concentrating on it, and no longer expanding it quickly. That fits the chart: the frontier is still ahead, and buyers seem to be paying for it while looking harder at what each extra point costs.

6. Which one should you use?

  1. High volume, ordinary difficulty. Start with the cheapest model that passes your own tests, and include small proprietary models in that comparison, not only open ones. On the new index, Haiku-class and Terra-class models beat the cheapest open models on both score and cost per task. Pick open weights here when you need them for a reason the benchmark does not measure: data that cannot leave your servers, guaranteed prices, or fine-tuning.
  2. Hard reasoning on a budget. Compare the mid-priced proprietary models at high effort against the flagships at lower effort. GPT-6.1 Sol's score at an eighth of Opus 5.5's cost per task is the clearest sign that the expensive default is often not the best value.
  3. You need the very top. Today that is Claude Fable 5.2, then Gemini 4 Carbon and Claude Opus 5.5. Measure the cost per task on your own workload, not the list price, because the most capable model is also by far the most expensive to run on this test: $26.54 per task for Fable 5.2, against $5.48 for Gemini 4 Carbon.
  4. In every case, test it yourself. An index point is an average over someone else's tasks. My standing bench runs the same tasks four times per model from a clean context, and single runs routinely flip a pass into a fail. Nothing in a leaderboard replaces running your own work.
